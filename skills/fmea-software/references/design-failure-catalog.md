# Design failure catalog

The failure modes this skill offers a design-side analysis, one section per element kind, as rows to be rewritten for one named element and one named function. [skill-authored]
It is loaded on demand during the failure-analysis step; nothing here enters an analysis unrewritten. [skill-authored]
Every row rests on one verified record or is marked as the skill's own, and the Provenance cell is the only place a row's tag lives. [skill-authored]

## Reading a row

A row is: a stable row id, the mode in the source's own terms, its typical effects, its causes, the controls that bear on it, a trigger or exposure note, and one provenance tag. [skill-authored]
The row id has the form `cat-<kind>-<NN>` with a two-digit `NN`, and an analysis copies the id and the tag verbatim into `chains[].catalog_refs[]`, so both are fixed rather than editorial. [skill-authored]
A Provenance cell holds exactly one tag. [skill-authored]
When a row rests on more than one record, the extra records are named inside the row's own cells as `also C0nn`, never as a second bracket. [skill-authored]
Effects are written at three levels — the element itself, its immediate consumer, and the end user — because that is the shape the analysis records and the level Severity is rated on is the last one. [skill-authored]
The end-effect vocabulary the rows reuse is Gunawi et al.'s six outage-impact categories, and the top of the range is Yuan et al.'s catastrophic-failure criterion; both are stated in the rows that carry them rather than repeated per row. [cites:C058] [cites:C030]
Controls are written as prevention, detection or compensating. [skill-authored]
The third slot exists because Neufelder's second edition keeps three constructs apart: the preventive measure, the compensating provision, and the corrective action. [paraphrased:C120]
The trigger or exposure note is what a chain built from the row copies into its `trigger` field, so a row that names one leaves the analysis with a trigger already in hand. [skill-authored]

## What counts as a failure mode here

A failure mode is a problem that degrades one or more components or makes them unavailable, and it is not the same thing as an error: an error is something the system routinely expects, absorbs on the spot, and carries on from at undiminished capacity, whereas a failure arrives unexpectedly and takes either intervention or a design built for that class of failure. [paraphrased:C011]
Only the first belongs in this catalog. [skill-authored]
No closed software failure-mode taxonomy survived verification: the ten-type Common Defect Enumeration claim was refuted, and so were both NASA claims that a two-table event-and-data fault taxonomy could be taken as one. [cites:C101] [cites:C107] [cites:C128]
So each row carries the source's own terms and no taxonomy label this skill invented, and a chain rewrites the row for its element rather than filing the element under a type. [skill-authored]
Prune before you apply: Neufelder's procedure is to remove from the list everything the system does not contain, then analyse the specification and design package as a whole against what remains. [paraphrased:C103]
Her deck rejects the hardware-style pair of modes — the software item fails to execute, the software item terminates — as accounting for under one percent of software failures, and rejects line-by-line analysis because very few failures come down to a single line of code. [paraphrased:C103]
Nygard's stability chapter in *Release It!* is organised under the section titles Failure Modes, Stopping Crack Propagation and Chain of Failure. [paraphrased:C048]
Reading those three as the same cause-to-mode-to-effect chain and the same containment controls this catalog is written in is the skill's own mapping rather than Nygard's. [skill-authored]
Nygard never invokes FMEA. [cites:C048]

## The two tiers

Tier 1 is the sourced rows: each rests on a verified record and carries that record's tag. [skill-authored]
Tier 2 is the one skill-authored set, the `security_component` rows, which rest on no source and say so. [skill-authored]
The two tiers are kept in separate top-level sections so a reader can tell at a glance which rows the evidence base supports. [skill-authored]
Three kinds — `interface`, `event_stream` and `datastore` — carry elicitation questions and no rows in either tier, and ML or LLM elements get the same treatment inside the `component` section. [skill-authored]
Sourced rows fall into `service`, `external_dependency` and `component` because that is where the evidence sits: the behavioural modes are service dynamics, the dependency modes come from the dependency-classification and platform records, and the error-handling and latent-defect modes are properties of code. [skill-authored]
A mode that crosses kinds is filed once, in the section where its record placed it, and cross-referenced from the others; the cross-reference lines below name every such row. [skill-authored]
Rows drawn from the Google SRE Book carry `cites:` and nothing else, because the chapter is CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 and its records permit no other tag. [cites:C040]

## Tier 1 — sourced rows

### `service`

Placement: cascading failure, retry amplification and metastable failure are modes of a service's own dynamics under load, and the outage effect and cause types are stated about services, so all four sourced groups land here. [skill-authored]
Convention for `cat-service-01`: the amplification is in the mode, the triggering event is in the causes, and the trigger note repeats that triggering event, so a chain built from the row fills `trigger` and the Occurrence lint does not fire on it. [skill-authored]
Convention for `cat-service-02`: the sustaining mechanism goes in the causes and the initiating event goes in the trigger note, never the other way round. [skill-authored]
Cross-references: a control that becomes the cause is filed here as `cat-service-06`, and `cat-component-04` under `component` cross-references it rather than repeating it. [skill-authored]

| Row | Mode | Effects | Causes | Controls | Trigger or exposure | Provenance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `cat-service-01` | Cascading failure. The mode is the amplification, not the first loss of capacity: the element's own degradation returns to it as further demand, the feedback is positive, and so there is no partial level the element settles at once the loop is closed. Record the amplification here and the capacity loss in the causes; a chain that names only the loss has recorded a cause and no mode. | Local: the element's remaining margin is consumed by the loop, so it stops serving altogether rather than at a reduced level. Next level: the flow that depends on the service stops. End: full outage. Gunawi et al. supply the end-effect vocabulary — full outage, failure of essential operations, performance glitch, data loss, data staleness or inconsistency, security attack or breach (also C058). | Name the triggering event that removed capacity or shifted load onto the survivors; the chapter behind C038 is the record that enumerates triggering conditions for this mode (also C038). Gunawi et al. find load-related outages originating in user request spikes, code upgrades, misconfiguration and flawed recovery (also C059). | The controls belong to the amplification, not to the trigger. Prevention: the policy changes Bronson et al. record for use while overloaded — turn retries and failover off, cap retries with a budget, serve queues last-in-first-out, shrink internal queues, rank requests by priority, reject a share of traffic, and break the circuit (also C035). Detection: alert on characteristic metrics such as queueing delay, latency, timeout rates and thread or connection counts (also C035). | Repeat the triggering event named in the causes; a cascading chain's `trigger` is its triggering cause restated. | [cites:C036] |
| `cat-service-02` | Metastable failure. After a trigger the service stays in a bad state with goodput too low to be usable, and stays there once the trigger is gone, because a sustaining effect — usually work amplification or lost efficiency — holds it. Bronson et al. exclude anything that clears when the trigger clears, naming denial-of-service, limplock and livelock as outside the mode. | Local: goodput near zero while the processes stay up. Next level: callers time out and retry, feeding the loop. End: an outage that survives removal of its cause, exited only by a strong corrective push such as a reboot or a dramatic load reduction. | The sustaining loop, not the trigger: Bronson et al. treat the loop as the root cause because many different triggers reach the same state, and outages are commonly misattributed to the trigger (also C033). Mechanisms they record from production: request retries; request failover spreading contagion across differently sharded replicas; look-aside cache loss turning into query amplification; slow error handling such as stack traces, DNS lookups and blocking log writes; link imbalance from a most-recently-used connection-pool policy. In nearly every case the thing that sustains the state is some resource running out, and the loop that drains it is usually built by a feature added to make the steady state more efficient or more reliable (also C034). | Prevention: retry budgets, disabled retries and failover under overload, LIFO scheduling, smaller queues, priority enforcement, load shedding, the circuit breaker pattern, autoscaling headroom, fast error paths, outlier hygiene. Detection: production-scale stress testing and alerting on characteristic metrics — queueing delay, latency, cache hit rate, timeout rates, thread and connection counts. Bronson et al. put the mode outside the reach of a unit or integration test, since it is behaviour that emerges rather than a logic bug, and they hold that stress tests at small scale buy little confidence, because the strength of the loop moves with constant factors (also C035). | Name the initiating event — a network blip, a cache loss, a load spike. Exposure is position in the three-state lifecycle of stable, vulnerable and metastable: hidden capacity is where stable ends and vulnerable begins, advertised capacity tops the vulnerable band as the nominal serving limit at which the system is already as vulnerable as it gets, and trigger intensity is the size of push needed at a given position. Systems run vulnerable for months or years, often deliberately, because it is more efficient than running stable (also C032). | [adapted-from:C031] |
| `cat-service-03` | Retry amplification. Independent retry policies at successive layers compose, so the load reaching the lowest element is not the load its caller issued. The mode is stated over the whole call path, because no one layer's policy is faulty by itself and no one layer can see the total it contributes to. | Local: the lowest layer, often a datastore, saturates. Next level: every caller above it slows or errors. End: an outage that outlives the transient fault that started it, because the retries keep the load on. | Retry logic present at more than one level, each level unaware of the others. Retry is also one of the sustaining mechanisms Bronson et al. record for `cat-service-02` (also C034), and Gunawi et al. count client retry floods among the recovery storms that flawed recovery produces (also C059). | Prevention: Bronson et al. name retry budgets and the disabling of retries under overload as overload-time policy changes, with the circuit breaker pattern as the client-side stop (also C035). Detection: alert on timeout rates and queueing delay at the layer that is absorbing the amplification (also C035). | Any transient failure or slowdown at the bottom of the stack arms it, so exposure runs at the rate of those transients and rises with the number of independently retrying layers. | [cites:C037] |
| `cat-service-04` | Outage of the service, whole or partial. Gunawi et al., over 597 unplanned outages across 32 services, break the impact into six categories: full outage 59%, failure of essential operations 22%, performance glitch 14%, data loss 2%, data staleness or inconsistency 1%, security attack or breach 1%. | Local: the service stops serving, or stops serving an essential operation. Next level: dependent flows fail or degrade. End: the six categories above are the effect vocabulary; the top of Severity is Yuan et al.'s criterion, a failure preventing all or a majority of users from normal access, or losing all or a majority of user data, with degraded-but-functional states excluded (also C030). | Gunawi et al. report that an outage commonly carries several root causes at once, with UPGRADE and BUGS, UPGRADE and NETWORK, CONFIG and LOAD, BUGS and LOAD, and HUMAN with UPGRADE or CONFIG their highest-correlated pairs, so this row is normally the end of a chain rather than a row on its own (also C059). | Compensating, in the proportions Gunawi et al. found reported: repairing software 22% and hardware 22%, restoring data 14%, doing nothing at all because a cross-dependency left no option 12%, adding resources 10%, rolling a release back 8%, correcting a configuration 7%, and restarting what was affected 4%. Only 24% of public reports disclose a fix procedure at all. | The row is normally the terminus of a chain, so its trigger is the triggering event of the row that caused it, restated here; where no other row precedes it, the exposure is the share of the flow the outage removes — the whole of it for a full outage, one essential operation for a partial. Severity cannot be inferred from the cause category: Gunawi et al. give per-root-cause median downtimes of 1.5 to 24 hours, with almost every category exceeding 50 hours at maximum, and every root cause recurring in nearly every year. For occurrence, treat public postmortems as sparse and biased — 60% of public reports give no root cause (also C060). | [adapted-from:C058] |
| `cat-service-05` | Recovery storm. The recovery itself generates extreme load: recovery that has gone wrong puts additional traffic onto the very resources it needs, and the congestion that follows calls for still more recovery traffic. Gunawi et al. record client retry floods, re-mirroring storms, simultaneous virtual-machine reboots, and denial-of-service protection whose CAPTCHA prompts more retries. | Local: the recovering service is saturated by recovery work rather than by useful work. Next level: recovery of the flow stalls or reverses. End: the outage lengthens past the fault that caused it. | Flawed recovery is one of the four sources of load-related outage Gunawi et al. name, alongside user request spikes, code upgrades and misconfiguration; the positive feedback loop is the mechanism. Failover cold-cache load spikes belong here too (also C057). | Prevention: cap the recovery's own load with a retry budget and load shedding on the recovery path, and stagger restarts rather than issuing them together (also C035). Detection: alert on the recovery path's own request rate, not only on the service's. | The initiating event is the start of recovery, so exposure is one exposure per recovery attempt, not one per unit of running time. | [adapted-from:C059] |
| `cat-service-06` | A detection or recovery control becomes the cause. An automated responder fires on a signal that overload also produces, and the response removes capacity from an element that was still doing partial work, so the automation is a cause in a chain of its own rather than a control on someone else's. | Local: capacity is removed from a service that was still doing partial work. Next level: the flow loses the redundancy the design counted on. End: the outage the automation was bought to prevent. | Overload read as unhealthiness by an automated responder. Gunawi et al. find redundancy does not remove single points of failure, because the failure recovery chain — complete detection, flawless failover code, working backups — is itself fallible, and they report defects turning up in the monitoring and error-checking code itself (also C057). | Prevention: bound or suspend the automated response while the service is overloaded. Detection: give the control its own chains, since it is an element in its own right. Redundancy on its own does not justify a low Occurrence or a low Detection rating (also C057). | Exposed whenever the automated response can fire on a signal that overload also produces. This row is the filing for control-as-cause; the component-side view of a defective recovery chain is `cat-component-04`. | [cites:C039] |

### `external_dependency`

Placement: these rows are about something outside the analysis boundary that the flow needs, which is exactly the dependency-classification material in the Azure records and the recovery-chain material in the Gunawi records. [skill-authored]
Cross-references: cascading failure is filed as `cat-service-01` and applies to a dependency that shifts load onto its own survivors; metastable failure is filed as `cat-service-02` and applies when the sustaining loop lives in the dependency; retry amplification is filed as `cat-service-03` and applies to retries aimed at a dependency. [skill-authored]

| Row | Mode | Effects | Causes | Controls | Trigger or exposure | Provenance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `cat-external_dependency-01` | The dependency is unavailable, or answers more slowly than the availability SLA and scaling limits recorded for it. | Local: calls to the dependency time out or queue. Next level: a strong dependency, one the flow can neither function nor stay available without, takes the flow down; a weak one costs only the features that reach for it. End: full outage for a strong dependency, degraded function for a weak one. | The recorded availability SLA is not met, or the recorded scaling limit is reached. Azure's method captures both for internal dependencies during structure analysis, alongside the internal-or-external and strong-or-weak classification, so the mode is stated against a number the analysis already holds. Platform-level causes are filed separately at `cat-external_dependency-02` (also C013). | Prevention: take the dependency off the critical path, or reclassify it from strong to weak by making the flow complete without it. Detection: monitor the dependency's responses against the SLA that was recorded, not against a nominal one. Compensating: Bronson et al.'s circuit breaker and load shedding apply once the dependency's slowness is feeding a loop (also C035). Take failing reads and failing writes as two cases, since neither their consequences nor their remedies are the same (also C013). | Exposure is the share of the flow's work that must reach the dependency, and how close the flow runs to the dependency's recorded scaling limit. | [adapted-from:C014] |
| `cat-external_dependency-02` | Platform-level outage beneath the dependency. The dependency is sound and its platform is not: a regional outage, an availability-zone outage, or an outage of a platform service. Azure's list sets these three beside five more: an operator's mistake, a component or application configured wrongly, an overloaded component, a maintenance outage that was planned, and a denial-of-service or other malicious attack. It is written as an inclusion rather than as a closed set. | Local: the dependency stops answering across a whole zone or region at once. Next level: every flow through it fails together, and any failover target inside the same zone or region fails with it. End: full outage of the flows that need it. | One of the eight environment-level items above. This list carries no software-behaviour modes — no faulty timing, retry storm, poison message or idempotency violation — so a chain that needs one of those takes it from another row rather than from here. | Prevention: the structure work that makes the shared layer visible — decompose by component type — compute, storage, the data layer, networking, the controls on the way in and on the way out, and the supporting services such as authentication, messaging and key or secret management — and classify each dependency strong or weak (also C014). Compensating: placement that does not put the primary and its backup behind the same platform boundary. | The initiating event is the platform outage. Exposure is the number of components sharing that region, zone or platform service, counted separately for reads and for writes. | [adapted-from:C013] |
| `cat-external_dependency-03` | Failover to or away from the dependency is cold or contagious. The replacement starts empty, or the failover itself spreads the failure. Bronson et al. record request failover producing cascading contagion across differently sharded replicas, and the loss of a look-aside cache turning into query amplification: a cache running at a 90% hit rate, once lost, multiplies the queries reaching the store behind it tenfold. | Local: the replacement is driven past its capacity by the shifted load. Next level: the flow's latency does not recover after failover. End: an outage that survives the failover meant to end it. | The sustaining mechanism is the amplification the failover creates, not the original dependency fault, and it is the amplification that must be recorded as the cause (also C033). Bronson et al. report a worst case of one-hundred-fold work amplification in a geo-distributed system, and put it generally: what holds the system in the state is nearly always a resource running out, drained by a loop that some steady-state efficiency or reliability feature built. Gunawi et al. list buggy failover code and failover cold-cache load spikes among the flaws in the recovery chain (also C057). | Prevention: a failover budget and the disabling of failover during overload (also C035). Compensating: warm the replacement before shifting traffic to it. Detection: alert on cache hit rate and on the query rate of the store behind the cache (also C035). | The initiating event is the dependency fault or the cache loss. Exposure is the hit rate the cache carries in steady state and the capacity headroom of the failover target. | [adapted-from:C034] |
| `cat-external_dependency-04` | Reconnection stampede. When the dependency returns, every client reconnects at once and knocks it down again. Gunawi et al. record post-outage reconnection stampedes as one of four hidden single-point-of-failure classes in the failure recovery chain, alongside undetected failures, buggy failover code, and backups that also fail. | Local: the recovered dependency is saturated by connection setup rather than by useful work. Next level: recovery of the flow is delayed or reverses. End: the outage lengthens. | Clients retrying in lockstep, with no dispersion between them; the same positive feedback loop Gunawi et al. call a recovery storm (also C059). | Prevention: a retry budget and load shedding on reconnect, with a circuit breaker keeping a client out of the stampede (also C035). Detection: alert on connection-establishment rate at the dependency, distinct from request rate. | The initiating event is the dependency's return to service. Exposure is the number of clients holding a connection and how tightly their retry timing is correlated. | [adapted-from:C057] |

### `component`

Placement: these rows are properties of code and of the artifacts code is written from, which is where the error-handling records and the latent-defect record sit. [skill-authored]
Cross-references: cascading failure is `cat-service-01`, metastable failure is `cat-service-02` and retry amplification is `cat-service-03`, each filed once under `service`, so a component that participates in one of them is analysed against the filed row rather than a copy of it. [skill-authored]
A control that becomes the cause is filed under `service` as `cat-service-06`, and `cat-component-04` cross-references it, covering instead the recovery chain analysed as an element in its own right. [skill-authored]

| Row | Mode | Effects | Causes | Controls | Trigger or exposure | Provenance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `cat-component-01` | Incorrect handling of a non-fatal error the software itself signalled. The component raises an exception or returns an error, and the handler that receives it does the wrong thing. Yuan et al., across 198 user-reported failures sampled from Cassandra, HBase, HDFS, Hadoop MapReduce and Redis, found this behind 92% of the 48 catastrophic failures, against 25% of the non-catastrophic ones. | Local: the component continues on a state the error should have stopped. Next level: the fault crosses the boundary that was meant to contain it. End: Yuan et al. set the catastrophic bar at a failure that blocks normal use for all or most users — an outage across the whole cluster, a cluster left hanging, or the loss of all or most user data — and keep out of it states that are degraded yet still functional, under-replicated blocks among them, even where every block is affected; 48 of 198 sampled failures met that bar although all five systems had high-availability mechanisms in place (also C030). | The defect is in the error path, not the happy path, so the cause is recorded against the handler and its origin is code or design. | Prevention: review and test of the error-handling path itself. Detection: in Yuan et al.'s sample 76% of failures emitted an error message naming the failure and 84% had every one of their triggering events written to a log, against a noisy median of 824 log messages per failure; a unit test could reproduce 77% of them, and 98% showed up on three nodes or fewer. Small-scale tests and the logs already being written are therefore both credible detection controls here (also C029). | The trigger is the signalled error the handler receives, and behind it the initial fault that signals it: Yuan et al. describe these as chains of more than one fault, in which an initial fault — a hardware fault, a misconfiguration or a bug — first shows itself as a non-fatal error. Name both on the chain. | [adapted-from:C026] |
| `cat-component-02` | A trivial error-handler defect. The handler has nothing in it, or nothing but a line that logs; or it catches something far too broad and takes the whole cluster down on it; or it is still carrying a TODO or a FIXME. Yuan et al. found these three patterns behind 35% of catastrophic failures. | Local: the error is swallowed, or a recoverable error is escalated into a process or cluster abort. Next level and end: as `cat-component-01`. | Specification or design: the handler was left as a placeholder, or catches at a breadth that hides the case it then aborts on. | Detection, as two distinct layers with measured yield. A static checker reaches these patterns with no runtime context: Aspirator, the checker Yuan et al. built, flagged 379 bad practices and 121 previously unknown bugs across nine widely used distributed systems, of which the developers fixed or confirmed 143, with 19% of its warnings false positives. Testing that merely reaches every statement of the error-handling code accounts for a further 23% of catastrophic failures, putting the two layers together at 58%. Keep them as separate controls, because they carry different evidence. | The defect exists from the moment the handler is written; exposure is the rate at which the guarded error is signalled. | [adapted-from:C028] |
| `cat-component-03` | A latent defect that a particular input or state reveals. The component does not wear out: it is either working or already defective with nobody aware of it, and software faults do not usually appear as a hard lockup the way hardware failures do. NASA's handbook takes a failure mode to be the kind of defect behind a failure and the form that failure takes when it shows itself, and its two software examples are a data value outside its limits and data arriving garbled. | Local: a wrong or garbled value leaves the component. Next level: the consumer acts on it. End: whatever the consumer's action reaches, up to the catastrophic bar recorded at `cat-component-01`. | The defect lives in the specification or the design, which is where Neufelder puts the origin of software failure (also C103). | Prevention: specification and design review, since that is where the defect was introduced. Detection: test for the revealing condition rather than for the defect. | The trigger is the input or the state that reveals the defect; name that input or state on the chain. | [paraphrased:C127] |
| `cat-component-04` | The failure recovery chain is itself defective. The component that detects failure, the failover code, or the backup component fails in its turn. Gunawi et al. document four hidden single-point-of-failure classes: undetected failures such as memory leaks, spikes of a request type nobody monitors, and expiring certificates; buggy failover code, including recovery that produces a recovery storm and failover cold-cache load spikes; backups that also fail, through coincident multiple failures or through cascading bugs, where one body of logic runs on every replica that was only assumed to be independent and every copy of it takes the same crash path; and post-outage reconnection stampedes. | Local: the control does not act, or acts wrongly. Next level: the redundancy the design counted on is absent at the moment it was needed. End: an outage the design treated as impossible. | Bugs in monitoring and error-checking code; one body of software logic running on replicas that were only assumed to be independent; a failure class nobody monitors for. | Give the detection control and the failover path their own elements, functions and chains, rather than listing them only as controls on someone else's row. Redundancy alone justifies neither a low Occurrence nor a low Detection rating. | Exposed exactly when the primary fails, which is the moment the chain has never been exercised at that scale. When the control causes the failure by acting on a signal overload produces, the mode is filed at `cat-service-06` and analysed there. | [adapted-from:C057] |
| `cat-component-05` | Misconfiguration the rest of the ecosystem does not follow. A configuration change is made in one subsystem and not propagated to the others, so the subsystems end up disagreeing about which setting is the right one; Gunawi et al. call configuration ecosystem-dependent for exactly this reason. | Local: the component behaves to a setting nothing else shares. Next level: two components disagree about the same fact. End: failure of essential operations, or a full outage when the disagreement sits on a critical path. | A change applied to one subsystem alone. Because Gunawi et al. find CONFIG correlating with LOAD and HUMAN correlating with CONFIG among the highest-correlated root-cause pairs, this mode normally appears inside a multi-cause chain rather than alone. | Prevention: propagate the change across the ecosystem as one unit. Detection: check for conflicting views between subsystems, not only for the change itself. Compensating: rollback, which Gunawi et al. record at 8% of reported fix procedures against fixing the misconfiguration at 7% (also C058). | The initiating event is the change. Exposure runs from the moment the change lands until the ecosystem agrees again. | [adapted-from:C059] |

#### ML or LLM elements: elicitation questions, no rows

No record in the evidence base supplies failure modes for a machine-learning or large-language-model element. [skill-authored]
The one claim that offered a machine-learning category inside a software failure-mode enumeration failed verification, so nothing is taken from it. [cites:C101]
Until a sourced run supplies rows, an ML or LLM element is analysed by answering these questions and writing the answers as ordinary modes on that element. [skill-authored]

- What does the element do when the model is unavailable, slow, or rate-limited, and which of those three has ever happened? [skill-authored]
- What output shape does the element accept, and what does it do with an output that does not match it? [skill-authored]
- What is the fallback when the model declines to answer or answers with low confidence, and who decided that threshold? [skill-authored]
- Which decisions does the model's output reach without a human or a deterministic check in between? [skill-authored]
- What does the element do with an output that is well formed and wrong, and how would anyone notice? [skill-authored]
- How is a change of model or model version detected, and what is re-tested when it changes? [skill-authored]
- Which part of the model's input is controlled by someone outside the trust boundary, and what does that make a cause of? [skill-authored]
- What does a repeated identical request produce, and does anything downstream depend on it producing the same thing twice? [skill-authored]

## Tier 2 — skill-authored rows

### `security_component`

These rows rest on no source: the evidence base holds no security failure-mode material, so the set is the skill's own, deliberately short, and every row is tagged `skill-authored`. [skill-authored]
Threat modeling itself is out of scope, and a cause whose agent is an adversary makes the chain a handoff rather than a row to be rated here. [skill-authored]
A security component is otherwise analysed as an ordinary element, with the same three-level effects and the same control kinds as every other row. [skill-authored]

| Row | Mode | Effects | Causes | Controls | Trigger or exposure | Provenance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `cat-security_component-01` | A path reaches the protected resource without the check running at all. | Local: the check is bypassed. Next level: the consumer receives data it was never entitled to. End: exposure of protected data or an unauthorised action. | A route, job, admin interface or internal caller added after the check was placed. | Prevention: enforce the check at one choke point rather than per path. Detection: assert on the negative case in tests, and count checks against requests in production. | Exposed on every path added after the control was designed; name the path. | [skill-authored] |
| `cat-security_component-02` | The check runs but decides on input the caller controls. | Local: the decision is attacker-chosen. Next level: privilege is granted that was never held. End: unauthorised action under a legitimate identity. | Identity, role or tenant taken from a request field rather than from the verified session. | Prevention: derive the decision only from verified state. Detection: test the check with a forged field. | Exposed on every request that carries the field; if the agent is an adversary, hand the chain to threat modeling instead of rating it. | [skill-authored] |
| `cat-security_component-03` | A credential, key or token is still accepted after it should have stopped being valid. | Local: an expired or revoked secret passes. Next level: a withdrawn grant keeps working. End: access persists past the moment it was withdrawn. | Revocation that is not checked, an expiry that is not enforced, or a cache of decisions that outlives them. | Prevention: bound the lifetime of every cached decision. Detection: alert on use of a secret past its expiry, and rehearse a revocation. | Exposed for the whole window between withdrawal and the next check; name that window. | [skill-authored] |
| `cat-security_component-04` | The control fails open: under load, timeout or internal error it admits the request instead of refusing it. | Local: the control is unavailable and does not say so. Next level: the protected resource serves unchecked requests. End: an authorisation gap that opens exactly when the system is already degraded. | An error path in the control that returns allow, or a timeout treated as success. | Prevention: make the failure path deny. Compensating: shed load ahead of the control rather than through it. Detection: exercise the control's own timeout and error paths. | Exposed whenever the control is slow or erroring, which co-occurs with the service modes filed under `service`. | [skill-authored] |

## Kinds that carry elicitation questions and no rows

The evidence base answers no interface, event, queue or data question, so these three kinds carry no rows in v1 and none is invented for them. [skill-authored]
The questions below are the skill's own, and an analysis writes the answers as modes on the named element rather than treating a question as a row. [skill-authored]
None of these questions carries a row id, because only rows carry ids. [skill-authored]

### `interface`

- What is the contract, where is it written down, and which side owns changes to it? [skill-authored]
- What does the caller do when a field it requires is absent, out of range, or of the wrong type? [skill-authored]
- What happens when the two sides are on different versions of the contract, in each direction? [skill-authored]
- Which fields are optional in the contract but required by the caller's logic? [skill-authored]
- Is the call idempotent, and what does a repeat of it do? [skill-authored]
- What does the caller do with an error the contract does not enumerate? [skill-authored]
- What is the timeout, who set it, and what does the caller do when it fires? [skill-authored]
- What does a partial response do — some fields present, some missing? [skill-authored]

### `event_stream`

- What ordering guarantee does the stream give, and does any consumer depend on a stronger one? [skill-authored]
- What delivery guarantee does it give — at most once, at least once, exactly once — and does the handler tolerate that guarantee? [skill-authored]
- What happens to a message the consumer cannot process, where does it go, and who reads that place? [skill-authored]
- What does the consumer do with a duplicate, and how would it know? [skill-authored]
- How far behind may the consumer fall before the lag matters, and what happens when retention is reached first? [skill-authored]
- Who can replay the stream, and what does a replay do to state downstream? [skill-authored]
- How does a producer change the message schema, and what does an old consumer do with a new message? [skill-authored]
- What happens when the consumer is faster than the producer, and when it is slower for a sustained period? [skill-authored]

### `datastore`

- What consistency model do the readers assume, and does the store provide it? [skill-authored]
- What recovery point and recovery time does the design assume, and has a restore actually been performed against them? [skill-authored]
- What happens when a write partially succeeds? [skill-authored]
- Which invariants does the store enforce, and which exist only in application code? [skill-authored]
- What is the growth rate against the capacity limit, and what fails first when the limit is reached? [skill-authored]
- How is a schema or data migration rolled back once it has run against real data? [skill-authored]
- Which reads sit on a critical path, and would a stale read there be noticed? [skill-authored]
- What does the store do under a load spike — queue, reject, or slow every caller equally? [skill-authored]

## What this catalog does not carry

No closed taxonomy of software failure modes, because none survived verification: the ten-type enumeration claim, the NASA guidebook two-table claim, and the NASA handbook two-worksheet claim were each refuted. [cites:C101] [cites:C107] [cites:C128]
No Google SRE Book text: the chapter is CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, so the rows that name it carry `cites:` and take no wording, selection or arrangement from it. [cites:C040]
No text from Yuan et al. or Gunawi et al.: neither has a license status in evidence, so their findings and category labels are used as facts with the source named in the row, and nothing of theirs is reproduced. [cites:C026] [cites:C057]
No severity or business-impact anchor from Release It!: the publisher's page claims, with nothing cited behind it, that four fifths of what a project costs over its life falls in production, so that figure is not used. [paraphrased:C050]
No rows for `interface`, `event_stream` or `datastore`, and none for ML or LLM elements, until a sourced run supplies them. [skill-authored]
